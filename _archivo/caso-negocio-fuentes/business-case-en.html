Container Detention CRM

Turning a runaway cost — USD 588k in 11 months — into a visible, managed, declining cost. And replicating the solution across Dow's operations in Latin America.

1.Executive summary

The problem. The operation's detention cost has tripled: from an average of USD 25k per month in 2025 to USD 77k per month in 2026, peaking at USD 117k in April. Accumulated over August 2025 – June 2026: USD 588,370. At the current pace, 2026 would close above USD 900k. The root cause is not operational but one of visibility: with manual spreadsheet tracking, a delay is discovered only when the invoice already exists.

The solution. An in-house CRM that follows every container from empty pickup to closure, computes the remaining free days per carrier daily, and raises alerts before the first dollar of charges. The platform is in its second version, deployed to production and validated against the operation's full history; internal test-mode rollout is starting now — execution risk is zero.

The opportunity. Between USD 118k and 206k per year of addressable savings (conservative and achievable scenarios), plus recoveries from disputing wrongly invoiced charges. Operating investment is marginal: minimal-cost cloud infrastructure, no per-user licenses.

The scale. The architecture is 100% parameterized — carriers, tariffs, free time, plants, depots and language are configuration, not code — and the interface operates in Spanish, Portuguese and English. The same system replicates to any Dow containerized export operation in Latin America, each with its own baseline.

The ask. Formal adoption of the system as the operation's single system of record, with a 4-week value-capture plan and monthly measurement of savings against the historical baseline already loaded.

2.The problem in numbers

Every container picked up from a depot has a free-time allowance granted by the carrier; once exceeded, a daily tariff in USD is invoiced per container. With 300–400 containers per month, four active carriers with different allowances and tariffs, and two plants with transfers between them, manual tracking stopped being enough. 3.The savings opportunity

Each avoided delay day is worth USD 33 on average (up to USD 85 per day depending on the carrier). Over the analyzed period, 17,565 delay days accumulated. Not all detention is avoidable — vessel delays and structural lags exist — but the share caused by lack of visibility is. Scenarios over the real baseline:

The savings mechanism is concrete: the system raises a yellow alert days before free time expires (configurable threshold) and a red alert on the first day of delay, with the accumulated cost and the cost of each additional day in plain sight. The container gets prioritized, shipped or returned earlier. The 16+ day band — 68% of the cost — is the first to disappear, because it stops being possible for a container to spend a month in delay without anyone seeing it.

Additional recovery line: every operation keeps an audited timeline (date, user and detail of each event), carrier-granted waivers are recorded with their net billable amount, and damage is documented with photos on the spot. That evidence is the basis for requesting waivers and disputing incorrect charges with the carriers — impossible to sustain with a spreadsheet.

Assumptions: scenarios computed over the 17,565 delay days of the Aug-2025/Jun-2026 period at the effective average tariff of USD 33.5 per delay day. They exclude dispute recoveries and man-hour savings.

4.The solution

The Container Detention CRM records each container's full life cycle: empty pickup at the depot (free time starts), transit and arrival confirmation at the plant, movements between plants, loading and assignment (booking, vessel, destination), departure by shipment or return, and terminal gate-in confirmation (free time stops and the operation closes). On top of that record, the system computes daily, per container, the remaining free days and the projected cost, using each carrier's current tariff and an urgency traffic light (green / yellow / red).

The design is not theoretical: it came out of analyzing a full year of real operation (2,804 containers, 417 pickup batches). That is why the system replicates how the team works — batch entry, automatic ISO 6346 validation of every container number, Excel export of any list — and not the other way around. Lists update in real time on every connected screen, and sensitive actions (voids, corrections) require the supervisor role and are logged with user and date.

6.Investment and return

  • Development: complete. No pending build investment.
  • Operation: minimal-cost cloud infrastructure (hosting and managed database), with no per-user or per-seat licenses.
  • Return: keeping just two containers per month out of delay covers the system's annual operating cost. Any additional saving — the Section 3 scenarios estimate USD 118–206k/year — is net.
  • Measurement: savings will be verified monthly with two indicators compared against the historical baseline already loaded: average delay days per container and percentage of operations closed within free time.

8.Built to scale: from Bahía Blanca to Latin America

Detention mechanics are identical in every port in the world: a carrier-granted free-time allowance and a daily tariff in USD per container. The same global carriers — Maersk, MSC, Hapag-Lloyd, ZIM — operate across the region with the same logic. Only each site's parameters change — and in this system the parameters are configurable data, not code. Wherever Dow exports in containers, free time is running, carrying the same risk Bahía Blanca already measured: USD 588k in 11 months.

Dow has a strong presence in Brazil — its largest footprint in the region — and also operates in the United States, Colombia, Peru, Mexico, Chile and Argentina, among other markets:

Bahía Blanca — today Brazil: Aratu · Guarujá · Santos Dumont · São Paulo · Hortolândia · Jacareí · Jundiaí · Breu Branco · Matarandiba Cartagena Lima Santiago Mexico City · Tlaxcala · Querétaro

What makes replication a matter of weeks rather than a new project:

  • 100% parameterized architecture. Carriers, tariffs and free time (versioned by validity date), day-counting rules, plants and depots are catalogs managed from the interface. Nothing about the Bahía Blanca case is hard-coded.
  • Multilingual out of the box. Interface and materials in Spanish, Portuguese and English — ready for teams in Brazil, Spanish-speaking America and the United States. This project's executive presentation already operates in all three languages.
  • A proven replication playbook. Onboarding a new site = loading local catalogs and rules + importing its history. That process was already executed in Bahía Blanca with 2,944 containers and 11,669 events. Estimate: 4 to 6 weeks per site, with the same value-capture plan as Section 7.
  • Marginal cost per site. No per-user or per-site licenses: the same cloud infrastructure scales, and each site measures its savings against its own baseline.
The prize of scale: the project's value does not end at Bahía Blanca's USD 118–206k/year. Every container-exporting site in the region replicates the measurement — with its own history as baseline — and captures its own savings with the same yardstick.
